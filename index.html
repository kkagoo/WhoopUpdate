Whoop Updated Its AI. My Chores Became Workouts.

My Whoop started counting chores as workouts. The explanation was buried in a model update.

A note on perspective. I've worn Whoop for a while and written a product teardown of it on this Substack. I've been recommending it. I also build SecondRise, a perimenopause fitness app, so I pay close attention to how wearables handle the difference between real training and everything else. This is about what happens when a company moves that line without saying anything.
01 The model changed. Whoop retrained its activity detection AI and lowered its auto-detection threshold. Users weren't notified.
02 Chores became activities. Mopping logged as walking. Cooking logged as functional training. Yard work logged as golf and field hockey.
03 The fix is manual. Whoop's FAQ says delete the ones you don't want. That's a weak answer for a $200/year athlete tracker.
04 The transparency gap. A public changelog exists. That's not the same as telling users their data is about to look different.

01  The Chores Became Workouts

Last week, Whoop started logging activities I didn't do.

Mopping the floor: walking. Grocery shopping: also walking. Cooking dinner: functional training. I turned off automatic activity detection. That fixed it for me, but I wanted to know what changed.

My uncle texted me around the same time. He'd been weed-whacking and Whoop logged it as golf. He's been working on his raised garden beds and Whoop filed that as field hockey. He picked fruit from a tree and Whoop gave him credit for jumping jacks. We laughed, because what else do you do when your wearable thinks yard work is field hockey?

02  The Model Moved

Whoop published a blog post on May 20 explaining how its activity detection works. The company says it "regularly re-trains" its activity detection and classification models, and that the latest release was "a step forward" in both the scale of data and the sophistication of the AI models behind workout detection. Recent improvements focus on lower-strain sustained activity, better functional-fitness detection, and fewer generic "Activity" labels.

Separately, Whoop's 2026 "What's New" page says that in January it lowered the minimum workout auto-detection duration from 15 minutes to 10. Whoop lowered the bar for what counts, then got more confident about naming it.

I didn't get an in-app heads-up. Whoop has a public changelog, but finding a changelog is not the same as knowing the model just redrew the line on what counts as your activity.

My best guess: weed-whacking looks enough like a golf swing. Bending over raised beds looks enough like field hockey. Reaching overhead for fruit looks enough like jumping jacks. That's my read, not Whoop's explanation.

"Lower Strain cardiovascular activities show up. You can delete any activity you don't want logged." — Whoop FAQ

"Delete the ones you don't want" is fine for a photo app. It's a weird standard for a product that asks me to plan recovery around its numbers.

03  Why That Matters

Whoop markets itself as an athlete tracker. I use Whoop because Strain is supposed to mean something. It's supposed to help me tell the difference between training load and life noise. Not errands plus chores plus whatever the model decided my uncle was doing with a weed-whacker.

That matters if you actually use the numbers. A fake walk here, fake field hockey there, and suddenly your week looks more athletic than it was.

I turned off auto-detection. I'll log manually. But I shouldn't have to make that choice because a model was retrained and deployed without any real communication about what was shifting. Whoop says it released updated models and its January changelog confirms it lowered the detection threshold from 15 to 10 minutes. I can't say exactly when those changes hit my account, but what I saw fits.

Whoop is good at a lot of things. This is the part companies still seem to underestimate: when an AI model changes inside a health product, users feel it in their own history. My data started telling a slightly different story. Whoop never really told me why. At $200+ a year, I expect the model to know the difference between a workout and a Tuesday.
